India's semiconductor policy is often described as a race to the leading edge. This paper treats it as a question of cost and infrastructure. It sets the India Semiconductor Mission, about $9.1 billion, beside the US CHIPS Act at about $52.7 billion and the EU Chips Act at about $43 billion. The incentive available to a given project is a much smaller share of the American programme, which decides the nodes that structure can support.

The argument is that mature nodes, roughly 28 to 90 nanometres, sit inside the cost structure, and a leading-edge node below 7 nanometres does not. An advanced fab draws on the order of 100 to 200 megawatts and a continuous water supply, and the scarce people are process engineers and doctoral specialists, not engineers in general. The Tata–PSMC fab at Dholera is the concrete case: an $11 billion plant aimed at about 50,000 wafers a month, with commercial production expected around 2028 or 2029. The recommendations follow the constraint. Secure power and water for clusters, train the missing specialists, and concentrate public money on 28 nanometres and above.
